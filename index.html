A visual guide to blood sugar

How your body handles glucose

Glucose is the sugar your cells burn for energy. Your body keeps the amount in your blood inside a narrow band, roughly 70 to 140 mg/dL, all day long. It does this with two hormones from the pancreas and a few organs that store, burn or release glucose. Pick a situation below and watch where the glucose goes.

    01

    From plate to blood

    Rice, roti, bread and potatoes carry starch, a long chain of glucose units. Fruit and sweets carry simpler sugars. Digestion snips starch into single glucose molecules. Glucose curls into a six-sided ring, which is why it is drawn as a hexagon here.

    • The small intestine absorbs glucose into the blood. That blood goes to the liver first, then to the rest of the body.
    • Table sugar is half glucose and half fructose. Fructose is handled mostly by the liver.
    • Fibre, fat and protein in a meal slow digestion, so glucose arrives more gently.

    Most digestible carbohydrate ends up as glucose in your blood. Fibre passes through undigested.

    02

    The pancreas: your control room

    The pancreas sits behind your stomach. Most of it makes digestive juices, but scattered through it are roughly 1 to 3 million tiny clusters (estimates vary) called the islets of Langerhans. They make up only 1 to 2 percent of the organ, yet they run your blood sugar.

    • Beta cells sense glucose directly. When it rises, they release insulin within minutes. Insulin is the "store it" hormone.
    • Alpha cells respond when glucose falls. They release glucagon, the "release it" hormone, which tells the liver to send glucose out.
    • The two work like a seesaw. As one goes up, the other usually goes down, which keeps sugar inside the safe band. A protein-rich meal is the exception: it raises both (chapter 13).

    Insulin brings blood sugar down. Glucagon brings it up.

    03

    Insulin is the key, GLUT4 is the door

    Glucose cannot just soak into most cells. It needs a doorway, a transporter protein. Muscle and fat cells keep their main glucose doors, called GLUT4, tucked inside the cell until insulin arrives.

    1. Insulin binds its receptor on the cell surface, like a key in a lock.
    2. The signal moves GLUT4 doors up to the surface.
    3. Glucose flows in. When insulin falls, the doors are pulled back inside.

    Insulin also tells the liver to stop releasing glucose and start storing it, and tells fat cells to stop releasing fat. The brain, red blood cells and liver use other doors (GLUT1, GLUT2, GLUT3) that do not need insulin.

    Without insulin, only a small trickle of glucose gets into resting muscle and fat.

    04

    Inside a muscle cell

    Once inside, glucose gets a phosphate tag (it becomes glucose-6-phosphate) so it cannot slip back out. Then it takes one of three routes:

    • Burn it with oxygen. Glycolysis splits glucose into two pyruvate molecules, which enter the mitochondria. One glucose yields roughly 30 ATP, the energy currency that powers every contraction.
    • Burn it fast. When glycolysis outpaces the mitochondria, as in a sprint, pyruvate becomes lactate. Only 2 ATP per glucose, but very quick. Lactate is then burned by the heart and other muscles, or rebuilt into glucose by the liver.
    • Store it as glycogen. Branched chains of glucose packed into the cell. Your muscles hold about 400 g in total, the largest glycogen store in the body.

    Muscle lacks the enzyme (glucose-6-phosphatase) needed to release free glucose back into blood. Its glycogen is a private reserve, for its own use only.

    Muscle is the biggest consumer and the biggest store of glucose, but it never shares.

    Next: the liver →
    05

    The liver: your glucose bank

    The liver is the only organ that both stores large amounts of glucose and gives it back to the blood. Think of it as a bank that takes deposits after meals and pays out between them.

    • Deposit. After eating, insulin tells the liver to soak up glucose and store about 100 g as glycogen. Extra carbohydrate is mostly burned in place of fat. The liver turns it into fat only when you regularly eat more energy than you use.
    • Withdraw. Between meals, glucagon tells the liver to break glycogen down and release glucose. These stores can hold blood sugar up for less than 24 hours.
    • Make new glucose. Called gluconeogenesis. The liver builds fresh glucose from lactate (from muscle), amino acids (from protein) and glycerol (from fat). After an overnight fast, the kidneys supply about a fifth to a quarter of the glucose entering the blood.
    • Make ketones. In long fasts or with no insulin, the liver turns fat into ketones, a backup fuel for the brain.

    Overnight, your blood sugar stays steady because the liver keeps paying out.

    Next: fat tissue →
    06

    Fat tissue: long-term storage

    Fat cells are mostly one big droplet of stored fat (triglyceride). Glucose plays a supporting role here, and insulin is the switch.

    • Insulin high (after meals). Fat cells open GLUT4 doors and take in glucose. They use it to make glycerol, the backbone that holds three fatty acids together as stored fat. They also capture fat from the blood. Insulin holds back the fat-release enzymes, so storage wins.
    • Insulin low (fasting, exercise). The lock comes off. Fat cells split stored fat into fatty acids, a fuel for muscle, heart and liver, and glycerol, which the liver can turn into glucose.
    • Fatty acids cannot be turned back into glucose. That is why the body guards its glucose for the brain and burns fat everywhere else when food is scarce.

    Fat holds many times more energy than glycogen. Insulin decides whether it fills or empties.

    Next: the brain →
    07

    The brain: the hungriest customer

    The brain is small but expensive to run. It stores almost no fuel and uses very little fat directly, so it depends on a steady stream of glucose from the blood, every minute of every day.

    • Its glucose doors (GLUT1 at the blood-brain barrier, GLUT3 on neurons) do not need insulin, so the brain gets fed even when insulin is low.
    • At rest, it uses about 60 percent of all the glucose the body burns.
    • In long fasts the liver makes ketones. After about 3 days they cover about a quarter of the brain's energy, and after several weeks they become its main fuel, sharply cutting its glucose need.
    ~2%of body weight
    ~20%of resting energy
    ~120 gglucose per day

    Every defence against low sugar exists, above all, to protect the brain.

    Next: exercise →
    08

    Exercise: a second key

    Working muscle has its own way to open the glucose doors. Contraction triggers signals inside the cell that move GLUT4 to the surface even when insulin is low. This is why movement lowers blood sugar, and why even a short walk after a meal blunts the rise.

    • During exercise, insulin falls and glucagon and adrenaline rise, so the liver releases more glucose to match what muscles take.
    • Muscles also burn their own glycogen and, in longer sessions, more fat.
    • Lactate from hard-working muscle goes to the liver, gets rebuilt into glucose and comes back. This loop is called the Cori cycle.
    • The doors stay more responsive to insulin for hours afterwards, as muscles refill their glycogen.

    Movement opens glucose doors without needing insulin.

    09

    When sugar runs low (hypoglycemia)

    Low blood sugar is most common in people treated with insulin or certain diabetes tablets (such as sulfonylureas), especially after a missed meal, unplanned exercise or alcohol. In people without diabetes, true lows are uncommon because the body defends in layers. Studies in healthy volunteers found these approximate trigger points:

    • About 80 to 85 mg/dL: the pancreas cuts insulin release. First line of defence.
    • About 65 to 70 mg/dL: glucagon and adrenaline surge, followed by cortisol and growth hormone. The liver pours out glucose.
    • About 50 to 58 mg/dL: warning symptoms. Shakiness, sweating, a pounding heart, anxiety, hunger.
    • Below about 50 mg/dL: the brain runs short. Trouble thinking, blurred vision, confusion. If severe: seizures or unconsciousness.

    Diabetes guidelines (ADA) call below 70 mg/dL Level 1, below 54 mg/dL Level 2 (clinically significant), and any low that needs another person's help Level 3. Repeated lows can blunt the warning symptoms, which makes the next one harder to notice.

    People with diabetes treat a low with fast-acting sugar as their care plan sets out, then recheck. Someone who cannot swallow safely needs emergency help or glucagon given by another person.

    The body raises the alarm in stages, and the brain feels it first.

    10

    When sugar runs high (hyperglycemia)

    High blood sugar happens when insulin is missing or not working well, so glucose piles up in the blood instead of moving into cells.

    In the short term

    • The kidneys normally take back every bit of glucose they filter. Above about 180 mg/dL their take-back pumps are full and sugar spills into urine.
    • That sugar drags water with it: frequent urination, thirst and dehydration. Tiredness and blurred vision are common too.
    • With almost no insulin (mainly type 1), fat breaks down so fast that ketones build up and turn the blood acidic. This is diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a medical emergency.

    Over months and years

    • Glucose sticks to proteins, a process called glycation. The HbA1c test measures sugar stuck to haemoglobin in red blood cells, which reflects your average over the past 2 to 3 months.
    • Glycated proteins damage small vessels (eyes, kidneys, nerves) and large ones (heart attack and stroke risk).

    Too much sugar does not feed cells better. It overflows and slowly damages.

    11

    Type 1 vs Type 2 diabetes

    Both end in high blood sugar, but they break the system in different places. Type 1 is a missing key. Type 2 is a stiff lock, with a key-maker that slowly tires.

    Type 1

    What breaks
    The immune system destroys the beta cells.
    Insulin
    Little or none.
    Typical start
    Often in childhood or young adulthood, but any age. Can come on over weeks.
    Main danger
    DKA if insulin is missed.
    Treatment
    Insulin for life, by injection or pump.

    Type 2

    What breaks
    Muscle, liver and fat respond weakly to insulin (insulin resistance), and over time beta cells cannot keep up.
    Insulin
    Present, often high early on, falling over the years.
    Typical start
    Usually adults, gradual, often silent for years. Linked to genes, extra weight around the belly, inactivity and age.
    Share of cases
    The large majority of all diabetes.
    Treatment
    Activity, weight loss and diet, plus medicines such as metformin. Some people later need insulin.

    12

    Muscle mass: your biggest glucose sink

    After a meal, skeletal muscle is where most glucose goes. In insulin-clamp studies, about 80% of glucose uptake happens in muscle. More muscle means more glucose doors and more room to store glycogen.

    • More muscle, better control. In a US national survey of 13,644 adults (NHANES III), every 10% increase in muscle relative to body weight was linked to 11% lower insulin resistance and 12% lower prevalence of prediabetes. This is an association measured at one point in time, not proof that adding muscle causes the change.
    • Too little muscle is a risk. Very low muscle mass is a known risk factor for insulin resistance.
    • Strength training helps. A 2023 meta-analysis of 26 randomised trials in people with type 2 diabetes found resistance training lowered HbA1c and fasting glucose, with the largest effects at 2 to 3 sessions a week. The ADA and the American College of Sports Medicine recommend combining aerobic and resistance exercise.
    • Protein supports the gains. In a meta-analysis of 49 trials in healthy adults doing resistance training, extra protein added muscle size and strength. Total intakes above about 1.6 g per kg of body weight a day gave no further muscle gain.

    More muscle gives glucose a bigger, more responsive place to go.

    13

    Eating protein instead of carbohydrates

    Protein (eggs, paneer, fish, chicken, meat, tofu) is digested into amino acids, not glucose. That changes how the system responds.

    • Little or no sugar rise. In single-meal studies, protein did not raise blood glucose in people with or without type 2 diabetes, even though the liver can turn amino acids into glucose.
    • Both hormones rise. Protein raises insulin and glucagon together, the exception to the seesaw in chapter 2. In people with type 2 diabetes, 50 g of protein from lean beef raised insulin about as much as 50 g of glucose did.
    • Swapping some carbs for protein. In a small 5-week study of 12 people with untreated type 2 diabetes, a higher-protein diet lowered blood sugar after meals and improved overall control. The ADA's 2019 consensus found no single ideal split of carbs, protein and fat for everyone, but reducing total carbohydrate has the most evidence for improving blood sugar in diabetes.
    • How much protein. The recommended dietary allowance for adults is 0.8 g per kg of body weight a day. For people with diabetes and kidney disease who are not on dialysis, kidney guidelines (KDIGO) advise about 0.8 g/kg and avoiding more than 1.3 g/kg, so targets should be agreed with a doctor.

    Cautions. Protein is not a treatment for low blood sugar: in type 2 diabetes it raises insulin without raising glucose (ADA). In type 1 diabetes, meals high in protein or fat can push sugar up several hours later, so checking glucose 3 or more hours after such meals is advised.

    Protein barely moves blood sugar, but it does move insulin and glucagon.

    Next: a balanced plate →
    14

    A balanced plate

    Most meals mix carbohydrate, protein, fat and fibre, and the mix changes the sugar response.

    • Company lowers the spike. In healthy volunteers, basmati rice, pilau rice and chapatti raised blood sugar less when eaten with chicken curry than when eaten alone. A study in healthy Korean adults found the same for a meal containing carbohydrate, protein and fat together.
    • Why. Fibre slows glucose absorption, fat slows how fast the meal is absorbed, and in people with diabetes protein boosts the insulin response.
    • Order matters. In 11 adults with type 2 diabetes, eating chicken, salad and vegetables 15 minutes before bread and juice lowered blood sugar by about 29%, 37% and 17% at 30, 60 and 120 minutes, with lower insulin, compared with eating the bread and juice first.
    • A simple template. The diabetes plate method used by the CDC and ADA: half the plate non-starchy vegetables, a quarter lean protein, a quarter grains or starchy food. On an Indian plate that could look like sabzi and salad; dal, paneer, egg or chicken; and roti or rice.
    • The overall pattern. The ADA's 2019 consensus found no single best eating pattern. What good patterns share: fewer added sugars and refined grains, more non-starchy vegetables, and whole foods over processed ones.

    Caution. Very high-fat meals can push blood sugar up later, which matters for people who dose insulin for meals.

    The same roti or rice raises blood sugar less when it shares the plate with vegetables, protein and fat, especially when they come first.
